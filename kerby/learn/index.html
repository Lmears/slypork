How KerBy works

How it started

KerBy started with the way I like to play: dividing a held note into pulses for a steady four-to-the-floor kick, then tweaking the rate every now and then for a stutter at the end of a phrase or a little glitch on the way back in. Testing with producers whose music worked differently led to Draw mode.

A note can become a rhythm.

Every MIDI note triggers both voices. In Live mode, hold a note and the Rate knobs repeat it.

By default the bass restarts at each kick, so it stays in step even when the two rates don’t line up. Switch off Restart on Kick to let it run across the bar as a polyrhythm.

In Draw, each note is a single hit, so make it long enough for the bass to come back after the kick.

Play one shots in time

Turn off the box beside each Rate knob and leave Q (Quantize) on. With your DAW playing, your hits land on the grid without repeating. Use the Rate knobs to choose the grid.

Go anywhere. Come home on the beat.

Push a stutter or drag the tempo around, then one press brings both Rate knobs and bend wheels home.

Macros, Glitch, Reverse and any sound knob you choose come back too: close a filter through a build and it opens on the drop.

Home is where the patch loaded, until you choose Set as home. Map Return Home to a controller button.

The shape is part of the sound.

The Wave tab draws one cycle of the oscillator: round for a soft tone, sharp edges for a harder one.

The other tabs draw change over time: how a hit fades, how its pitch falls, how the filter opens.

Start from a shape preset and move a few points, or paint steps with the drawing tool. The next pulse cuts a long shape short, so listen to Rate and Length together.

Look up the shape editor

Bring a recording into the pattern.

Drop a sample onto the Wave tab and it becomes that voice’s source, retriggered by the same rhythm and pitched by the notes you play.

To keep a synthesised kick and add a sampled attack, use the Click tab instead.

It works the other way too: drag a scope into your DAW, or onto a Wave tab, and the bar it shows arrives as a WAV.

Try it: layer a sampled attack Look up sample playback

Edit the bass pattern.

The bass step row decides which parts of the bar sound. Switch a box off for a gap; drag it up or down to change its pitch. Glide slides between steps.

Drag a step’s right edge for a longer note. The − and + set the pattern’s length, up to two bars; an odd length turns over against the bar.

Look up steps and their gestures

Make room for the kick.

Each kick triggers the duck curve, which controls the bass volume. Play around with it to find the right groove.

The kick’s reverb follows the same curve. Mute the bass to hear the reverb rumble between kicks.

Try it: find the space between the hits

Take the pattern somewhere else.

Pitch bend moves the notes; tempo bend pushes KerBy’s rhythm against the DAW’s. Reverse plays the finished sound backwards, duck and reverb included.

Glitch adds rolls and dropouts in time with the beat, from the odd stutter to rolls that change speed every bar.

On macOS, a PS5 controller plays it all with no setup: the sticks push Rate and bend, the triggers bring in Glitch, and the touchpad sends everything home.

Let one move drive many.

On the Matrix page, drag a macro onto knobs on either voice and one move reshapes both.

Draw an LFO, or morph it through sines, triangles, ramps and squares. Route one to Wave Shape and the oscillator itself moves.

Look up the Matrix

Keep both voices in key.

Pick a root and scale and every note KerBy plays lands in it, on both voices. The pitch envelope, Bend and the pitch wheel still sweep freely.

Put a random LFO on the bass’s Tune and it starts to sound like a bassline.
