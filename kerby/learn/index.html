The illustrated tour

Two voices, with a relationship.

The kick and bass each have a sound and a rhythm of their own. What makes KerBy useful is being able to work on the way they meet: when the bass starts, how it moves between notes, and how much room it leaves around the kick.

How it started

I built KerBy around the way I like to play: a steady four-to-the-floor kick, with a turn of Rate for a stutter at the end of a phrase or a little glitch on the way back in. Testing with producers whose music worked differently led to Draw mode. That’s where the instrument started; there’s no right rhythm or way to use it.

A note can become a rhythm.

Every MIDI note triggers both kick and bass. In Live mode, keep a note held, or play overlapping notes legato, and the Rate knobs do the repeating. A quarter-note kick with a sixteenth-note bass gives you four bass pulses for every kick. Turn Rate briefly for a stutter or repeat, then send it home to the original pulse. It stays visible on every tab so you can play the rhythm while editing the sound.

Restart on Kick can bring the bass rhythm back to its beginning at each kick. Switch it off to let the two rates run independently.

In Draw mode, write separate notes in your DAW for separate hits. The sound-shaping tools are the same; the notes decide when to use them.

In Draw mode, MIDI notes place the kick and bass together. Give each note enough duration for the bass to sound after the initial duck. To hear a faster divided bass rhythm, keep notes held or legato with Rate enabled; a short note won’t leave time for the later pulses.

Go anywhere. Come home on the beat.

Rate is there to be played, and the house button at the right of the bottom bar, beside the step row and keyboard buttons, is what makes that safe. Press it and both Rate knobs go back to their home and both bend wheels return to centre. Push a stutter as far as it goes or drag the tempo around, then bring the whole pattern back in one move.

Home starts as the rate the patch loaded with. To choose your own, right-click a Rate knob and pick Set as home; a small tick on the knob marks it while you’re away. Untick Goes home on a knob or wheel to leave it where it is while everything else returns.

Put it on a button you can hit without looking. Return Home is a plugin parameter, so use your DAW’s MIDI mapping to give it a button on your controller, mapped straight to the parameter. Each Goes home switch is a parameter too: map one to a button to take a control out of Return Home, or put it back, mid-set.

The shape is part of the sound.

The Wave tab draws one cycle of the oscillator. A rounded shape and a sharp-edged one give you different starting sounds before you touch an effect.

The other curves describe change over time: how a hit fades, how its pitch falls, how the bass filter opens, or how the bass returns after a kick.

Start with a shape preset, then move a few points. There is also a drawing tool for painting steps onto the grid. A long envelope may be interrupted by the next pulse, so Rate and Length are worth listening to together.

Bring a recording into the pattern.

Drop a sample onto the Wave tab and it becomes that voice’s source. The same rhythm now retriggers the recording. Played notes change its playback speed and pitch.

For a sampled attack over a synthesised kick, use the separate Click tab. The kick keeps its own waveform, and the sample gets its own level, envelope, high-pass filter and playback pitch.

It works the other way too. Drag either scope and the bar it shows drops into your DAW, or back onto a Wave tab, as a WAV. Zoomed in, you get the quarter bar on screen.

Leave holes. Change a few notes.

The bass step row decides which parts of the bar are allowed to sound. Switch a box off to leave a gap; drag it vertically to change the pitch there.

It works with the bass’s Rate, so changing the rate can change which parts of the pattern you hear. It is a way to shape the repeating rhythm while keeping your hands free to play.

The space is something you can shape.

The ducking curve is the bass’s volume after a kick. Low at the start leaves room for the hit; the rise brings the bass back in. Dial it in to find the desired groove.

The kick’s optional reverb follows that curve too. Try it with the bass muted for a low techno rumble that rises between kicks.

Take the pattern somewhere else.

Pitch bend changes the notes; tempo bend stretches or compresses KerBy’s rhythm against the DAW. Return Home brings both back to centre. Reverse works on a captured stretch of the finished sound, including the ducking and reverb.

There is more sound shaping in the More tabs: FM, Tone, Noise and Crush on both voices, with resonance and glide on the bass. The signal-flow map shows where these act.
